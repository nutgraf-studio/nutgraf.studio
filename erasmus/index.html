What's Erasmus

The European Community Action Scheme for the Mobility of University Students was adopted in 1987 and named after Erasmus of Rotterdam, the Renaissance scholar who studied and taught across Europe. In its first year, 3,244 students from 11 countries went abroad. Since 2014, as Erasmus+, it also sends apprentices, school pupils, teachers, adult learners and young people.

Why it is the EU's most iconic success. It turned European integration into something people live rather than read about: a term in another language, a flatmate from another country, sometimes a family. The Commission counts 16.6 million people sent abroad since 1987. In the spring 2017 Eurobarometer, a quarter of Europeans named student exchanges such as Erasmus among the EU's most positive results, and in 2014 the Commission estimated that around a million babies had been born to Erasmus couples. Forty years on, a whole generation is named after it.

Methodology and sources

What's on the map, what is not, and where the data comes from

What's on the map

1987/88–2013/14. The Erasmus programme for higher-education students: the European Commission's yearly totals of students who studied or trained abroad. Country-to-country flows are drawn for the six years whose home × host country tables survive in the Commission's archive (2004/05–2009/10). Outgoing and incoming totals per country are shown for 2000/01–2011/12. For 1987/88–1999/2000 each country glows with the students it sent abroad, from the Commission's table of outgoing students since 1987, which survives only in the Internet Archive.

2014–2024. Erasmus+ Key Action 1 (learning mobility of individuals) across all sectors: higher education, vocational education and training, school and adult education, youth and sport. Learners and staff are included. Years are calendar years of departure, and 2014 was the programme's launch year, so it is only partly covered.

Before 2014, the numbers cover university students only. The Higher education filter is the closest reading across the 40 years, though from 2014 it also includes university staff.

What is not

Where students went is not recorded per country before 2004/05 or for 2010/11–2013/14, and arrivals per country are not published before 2000/01, so no lines are drawn for those years. 2012/13 and 2013/14 have only Europe-wide totals. Nothing is estimated or interpolated.

The running counter adds up the participants in these files (11.9 million). The Commission's own headline, 16.6 million people since 1987 (at 1 April 2025, Commission report COM(2025) 395), is higher because it also counts the other education, training and youth programmes of 1987–2013 and Erasmus+ actions beyond Key Action 1.

Flows of fewer than three people, departures with no recorded country, and participants who stayed in their own country (for example youth exchanges hosted at home) are left off the map but kept in every total. Partner countries outside Europe appear on the globe; a few very small ones are grouped as “rest of world”.
